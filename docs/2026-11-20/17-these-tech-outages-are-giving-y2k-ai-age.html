These tech outages are giving Y2K, AI agent style

TechCrunch AI · 9 小时前
TechCrunch Image Credits: Muharrem huner / Getty Images Security These tech outages are giving Y2K, AI agent style Dominic-Madori Davis 1:42 PM PST · November 19, 2026 It’s going down, as the American rapper Yung Joc might say: the airline booking systems, banking platforms, and productivity platforms. In the past two weeks, the U.S.’s top businesses have faced technical outages that have disrupted the systems that run everyday life. Apple Pay went down. NASA’s internal communication network went down. The FAA had to ground all flights for hours (only the third time in history since 9/11), and Microsoft was hit with an outage that took down Outlook, Teams, and Copilot for nearly two days. The first thought, naturally, is an AI hacker invasion of some sort, especially after hackers managed to attack fridges in a grocery store and a blood bank in France a few weeks ago. Here in the U.S., the director of National Intelligence said these outages don’t seem related to state-sponsored terrorism, while President Trump called the issues “individual events.” Many of the companies themselves have said the outages were not caused by cyberattacks or AI attacks, but tech issues. Only, there’s the fact that they all struck within a short timespan of each other, and just a few weeks after Cybersecurity’s so-called “Black October,” when a slew of also-unrelated cybersecurity incidents occurred on October 21, striking PHP, Node.js, LiteLLM, more. While there’s no reason to view these outages as cyberattacks, there may be more than coincidence going on: It’s giving AI-native Y2K, frankly. Dave DeWalt, an investor who worked in cybersecurity for more than three decades (including as president and CEO of McAfee and as vice chairman of the CISA Advisory Council for the Department of Homeland Security), also thinks it’s probably AI-related. The technical outages are most likely the “growing pains of a security system that’s getting stronger, not weaker,” and that “AI is surfacing vulnerabilities at a speed and scale we’ve never had before through AI tools like Mythos and others,” he told TechCrunch. The thing is, many of these technical flaws have always existed, but because AI is surfacing them so fast, enterprises are rushing to fix them before bad actors can exploit them. “That surge in patching is disrupting things in the short term,” he said. This was, perhaps, the first moment when legacy meets modern, Anusha Iyer, a software engineer and founder of the security startup Corsha, told TechCrunch. “These industrial systems were never designed to be connected, let alone securely connected in the way we are pushing them to be today,” she said. Virginia Senator Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intel Committee, came to a similar conclusion (although investigations are still underway as to what could be happening). “These disruptions should serve as a wake-up call. It feels like every day brings another reminder that AI is moving faster than our ability to manage its consequences,” the senator wrote on X . AI is reshaping how software is built, deployed, and operated, on a scale where everyone feels the sharp learning curve. Iyer cited the FAA outage as an example of legacy tech that is not as sophisticated or advanced as the technologies companies are trying to merge it with.
